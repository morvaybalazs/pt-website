How to keep training when your week keeps changing

Balazs Morvay · 15 March 2026 · 5 min read

Most training plans assume every week looks the same. Yours does not. Meetings run over, a child gets ill, you travel, and the plan that looked fine on Sunday is in pieces by Wednesday.

The usual advice is to be more consistent. As a goal, "be consistent" is vague enough to be useless and demanding enough to make you feel like you are always failing. Here is what works better.

The problem with consistency as a goal

When people say they want to be consistent, they usually mean they want to stop feeling like they're failing. They've gone hard for two weeks, lost momentum, and watched everything fall apart. So they tell themselves: if I could just stay consistent, it would work out. The issue is that consistency is a byproduct: the result of a system that works, not something you can decide to be.

What actually works

The people who train for years without burning out aren't the most disciplined. They're the most strategic about what they ask of themselves.

  • Minimum viable doses. What's the least you could do and still progress? That is your floor on bad weeks, not the ceiling.
  • Built-in flexibility. Rigid programmes break. Flexible ones bend. Build around your actual schedule, not an idealised one.
  • Short recovery cycles. Miss a session? Fine. Get back next time. Don't catch up or punish yourself with extra volume.
  • Reduce friction. The best programme is one you can actually do. If it requires too much setup, it gets deprioritised eventually.

What this looks like in a real week

Say your plan is three sessions: Monday, Wednesday and Friday. On Wednesday a meeting eats your evening.

  • You do not double up on Friday. You do the Wednesday session on Friday, and the Friday session on Saturday or at the start of next week. The order of the sessions matters, the day of the week does not.
  • If the whole week goes, you carry on where you stopped, and everything after it moves back a week. You do not restart the plan.
  • If your week is short, not empty, you shorten the session instead of skipping it: the first two exercises, done properly, keep the progress you have.
  • If this keeps happening, the plan is wrong, not you. Two realistic days beat three you cannot make.

Aim for durability instead

If I had to replace "consistent," I'd use durable. A durable habit survives a bad week, a holiday, a cold, a change in circumstances. It does not need to be perfect. It just needs to still be there when things settle down. That's what we build toward: not an unbroken streak, but a practice that comes back naturally because it was designed to fit your life in the first place.
